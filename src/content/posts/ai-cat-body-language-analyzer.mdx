## What a cat analyzer can observe

Feline signals are distributed across the whole body. A useful analysis may consider:

- ears forward, neutral, rotated sideways or flattened
- eyes soft, narrowed or held wide, with pupil size interpreted against light
- whiskers resting, drawn back, pushed forward or changing asymmetrically
- head and neck extended, neutral or pulled toward the body
- body loose, upright, crouched, flattened or arched
- feet visible and relaxed or tucked firmly beneath a compressed body
- tail height, curve, piloerection and movement when visible
- weight and orientation toward or away from a person, animal, object or exit

No single marker settles the question. Dilated pupils can reflect low light, play, fear, excitement, medication or disease. Whiskers forward may appear in interested investigation and hunting focus. Ears rotated sideways become more informative when paired with a low body, tension and an exit-oriented stance.

## How the analysis works

### 1. Capture the real moment safely

Upload one clear photo or a short clip. Do not pull a cat from a hiding place, restrain them, move closer after a warning or recreate a conflict for the camera. Existing media is enough.

### 2. Add factual context

State what happened immediately before and after. "A delivery person entered; Juniper crouched behind the chair and stopped eating treats for two minutes" is useful. "Juniper looked angry" supplies a conclusion rather than evidence.

### 3. Review the visible markers

The report lists what can be seen before it offers a behavioral interpretation. If the tail is outside the frame or the eyes are hidden, that missing information should remain missing.

### 4. Use the confidence and action boundaries

PetTranslator returns observed markers, a possible interpretation, confidence, and a Do/Avoid plan. Ambiguous signals, poor lighting and incomplete framing should reduce confidence. Sudden medical changes and serious safety concerns should lead to professional referral.

## What the report includes

**Observation layer.** The visible ear, eye, whisker, posture, orientation and tail markers that can be checked against the upload.

**Interpretation layer.** A plain-language explanation of patterns the visible cluster may support, without claiming access to exact thoughts.

**Confidence.** An indication of signal agreement and input limitations. Confidence in image visibility is not the same as medical accuracy or certainty about emotion.

**Do/Avoid plan.** Low-risk, force-free next steps, such as preserving a hiding option, pausing touch, adding distance, lowering stimulation or gathering a better comparison.

**Referral boundary.** A reminder to seek veterinary or qualified behavior help where behavior changes suddenly, welfare is deteriorating or people and animals are at risk.

## How to take a useful cat photo

### Include the whole body

A face-only portrait hides the tail, feet, body compression and orientation. Step back or use an existing wider image. Keep the cat's escape route open.

### Use even, natural light

Backlighting hides eye and whisker detail. Flash can startle a cat and distort the eyes, so avoid it. Pupil size is especially unreliable when the lighting context is unclear.

### Keep ears, whiskers and tail in frame

Long fur can obscure muscle tension and leg position. A side or three-quarter angle often shows more than a straight-on portrait. For a tailless cat or a breed with unusual facial structure, state that context rather than treating the missing marker as behavioral.

### Show the environment when safe

A doorway, carrier, unfamiliar person, other cat or food bowl can explain what the cat is oriented toward. Context should never come at the cost of approaching a distressed cat.

### Compare with baseline

One calm photo and one concern photo can be more informative than either alone. The useful question is often what changed in the same cat: ear angle, body height, resting location, appetite or willingness to approach.

## When a short video is better

Video helps when meaning lives in the sequence:

- the tail changes from still to flicking during touch
- ears rotate before a cat retreats
- a crouch loosens after a visitor sits down
- two cats pause, exchange roles or continue a one-way chase
- the cat repeatedly enters and leaves a litter tray
- gait, jumping or transitions appear different

Recent research on human recognition of feline stress used clear full-body videos and still found that distinguishing relaxed, tense and fearful states was difficult. That supports a modest conclusion: good media and explicit markers can help, but visual information alone remains limited.

Never record a dangerous interaction instead of interrupting it safely. Never delay veterinary care to capture a better clip.

## What AI cannot determine from a cat image

### Exact emotion or intention

Posture can narrow possibilities without revealing a private sentence. A cat facing away may be resting, monitoring a sound, avoiding interaction or preparing to leave. The event sequence and baseline matter.

### Whether purring means contentment

Cats can purr during affiliative contact, self-soothing, stress and illness. A photo cannot assess sound, and sound alone does not settle emotional state.

### Whether the cat is in pain

Facial and postural changes can be reasons to seek veterinary assessment. They cannot identify the cause or rule pain in or out. Pain tools developed for clinical use have specific protocols and are not interchangeable with a general mood label.

### Whether an interaction is safe

No report can certify that a frightened, cornered or highly aroused cat will not scratch or bite. Give distance, preserve exits and use a barrier rather than hands when separation is necessary.

### Why behavior changed suddenly

Hiding, aggression, altered grooming, appetite loss and litter-box changes can have behavioral and medical causes at the same time. A veterinary history and physical examination provide information an upload cannot.

## Use AI to inspect, not declare

The strongest result is not "your cat is angry." It is a transparent chain: these ears, whiskers, eyes, posture and tail are visible; this context makes several interpretations more or less likely; this low-risk response preserves the cat's choice; this uncertainty requires more observation or professional help.

That turns an AI cat body language analyzer into a documentation and learning aid. It can make subtle signals easier to compare. It cannot replace living with the cat, tracking their baseline, or asking a veterinarian to examine a sudden change.
