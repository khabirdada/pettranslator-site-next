# Keyword brief: dog freezing body language

## Search intent

Informational with a safety component. The reader has seen a dog suddenly become motionless and wants to know whether it indicates fear, focus, pain or a warning before defensive behavior.

## Duplication audit

No dedicated freezing guide exists. `/blog/dog-stress-ladder`, `/blog/dog-body-language`, `/blog/signs-your-dog-is-stressed` and `/blog/dog-calming-signals` mention freezing within broader frameworks. `/blog/dog-resource-guarding` covers freezing over possessions. The new page should own the differential reading of stillness and provide context-specific responses.

Add a contextual link from the high-grade caution section of the broad body-language guide.

## Current result-page patterns

Current results are mostly broad body-language pages from PetMD, ASPCApro, VCA, Dogs Trust and Tufts, plus several recent low-authority pages. They describe freezing as fear or a warning and often say it comes directly before a bite. Research papers operationalize freezing as stopped movement or sound with muscle tension, sometimes in a low or rigid posture.

## Content opportunity

- Define a freeze as a change into tense immobility, not ordinary stillness.
- Distinguish fear-related freezing from alert orienting, predatory stillness, trained duration behavior, sleep/rest and pain or mobility hesitation.
- Avoid claiming every freeze predicts a bite.
- Teach readers to use onset, muscle tone, gaze, weight, mouth, trigger and recovery.
- Give separate action advice for handling, walks, dog-to-dog encounters and resource guarding.
- Cover “shutdown” carefully without diagnosing learned helplessness from appearance.
- Include urgent veterinary differentials such as collapse, seizure, weakness and acute pain.
- Emphasize that video is better than a single frame for detecting a transition into stillness.
